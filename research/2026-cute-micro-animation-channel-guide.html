Zero to Cute 🐾

This guide takes you from never having opened Blender to posting a cute 3D micro-animation (a 5–20 second Reel or Short with a lovable character and sound) every week. You can do it part-time. It covers the software, the skills, a character, the animation, the sound, the edit, posting and growth, and the money side, with a realistic take on each.

Written for an India-based solo builder doing this in spare time (about 6–8 hours a week). Everything the guide recommends you install is free.

Everything in this box = squash, stretch, anticipation, overshoot, a blink & a shadow

0 · The honest 60-second version

🎯 What you're building

One recurring original character (plus maybe a sidekick) in its own small world. It stars in short, looping, sound-driven gags. People follow the character, not the software.

🧰 What you need

A computer with a mouse, Blender (free), DaVinci Resolve (free), Audacity (free), headphones, and a phone for recording reference and sounds. You don't need to buy anything to start.

⏱️ How long

About 12 weeks at 6–8 hrs/week to get to your first good Reel. After that, with a reusable "studio file", a simple Reel takes 3–6 hours.

💸 Money reality

Expect no income for the first 6–12 months. In India, Instagram doesn't pay per view. The first real money usually comes from animating mascots for brands, not from ad revenue. See §19.

The one-sentence strategyMake one lovable character, post one short sound-driven loop every week for a year, study what gets sent to friends, and use the channel as a portfolio that brings in paid mascot and animation work. The earlier research in this repo (research/2026-solo-builder-economics.md) reached a similar conclusion: creator content works best as an audience and portfolio layer, not as your only income plan.

How to use this page: read §0–§5 today. Then follow the 12-week plan and use §6–§15 as the manual while you work. Checkboxes throughout the page are saved in your browser, and the bar in the sidebar tracks your progress.

2 · The science of "cute" (so you can design it on purpose)

Scientists call it "baby schema" (Kindchenschema): a set of features humans instinctively find adorable. Every cute channel follows it. Use it as a design checklist:

FeatureDo thisAvoid
Big headHead is ½ to ⅔ of total height, or the whole character is a head (blob style)Realistic body proportions
Big, low eyesEyes sit below the middle of the face, spaced wide, with 1–2 white highlight dotsSmall eyes high on the head, sharp eyelids
Round shapesCircles and soft ovals, bevelled everythingSharp corners, spikes (these read as "villain")
Tiny featuresTiny mouth, tiny or no nose, stubby limbsDetailed teeth, big noses, long fingers
Soft coloursPastels. 1 main colour + 1 accent + dark eyes + blush pinkMany saturated colours fighting each other
Soft materialsMatte, slightly glowing skin (subsurface), clay or felt lookShiny plastic, realistic fur (also very slow to render)
Clumsy, bouncy motionSquash & stretch, wobbles, little hops, slightly too-late reactionsStiff, robotic, perfectly even movement
Small soundsHigh-pitched squeaks, pops, pitter-patter feetDeep, heavy, realistic sounds
The thumbnail testShrink your character to the size of your thumbnail nail on the phone screen. Can you still tell what it is and what it's feeling? If not, simplify.

Shape language (quick rule): circles feel friendly and safe, squares feel sturdy and stubborn, triangles feel dangerous or energetic. A cute lead character is ~80% circles. A grumpy sidekick can be a bit square, and that contrast is funny.

6 · Blender first steps (absolute beginner)

6.1 Install

  1. Go to blender.org/download, choose your OS, and download 5.2.x LTS. On Windows, the installer (.msi) is simplest.
  2. Open Blender. You'll see a splash screen. Click outside it. The default scene has a cube, a camera and a light.
  3. Don't install add-ons yet. Everything in this guide is built in.

6.2 Change these settings first

Open Edit → Preferences:

  • Input → Emulate Numpad: turn it ON if your keyboard has no number pad (most laptops). The number row then works as a numpad.
  • Input → Emulate 3 Button Mouse: only if you truly don't have a mouse. (Get a mouse.)
  • Save & Load → Auto Save: ON, every 2 minutes. Blender can crash, and this saves your work.
  • Interface → Resolution Scale: raise it to 1.1–1.3 if the text is too small.
  • The preferences save automatically (or use the ☰ menu at the bottom left → Save Preferences).

This is the most important creative decision you'll make. The character is your brand, your logo, your merch and your portfolio piece.

7.2 The sketch process

  1. 20 thumbnails: tiny doodles (2–3 cm), 1 minute each, just silhouettes. Quantity first.
  2. Silhouette test: fill each one in solid black. Pick the 3 most recognisable.
  3. Refine 3: apply the baby-schema checklist. Try different eye sizes and positions. They change everything.
  4. Pick 1 and draw a turnaround: a front view and a side view, the same height, lined up. You'll load these into Blender as reference images.
  5. Expression sheet: the same face happy / sad / surprised / angry / sleepy. If you can't draw a clear emotion, the design is too complicated.
  6. Colour: pick 1 main colour + 1 accent + dark eye colour + blush. Save the hex codes.

7.3 Three difficulty levels (choose honestly)

Level 1 · recommended start

🫧 Blob

Body = head. No arms or legs, or tiny floating nubs. Moves by hopping, squashing and wobbling. Faces carry the emotion. Fastest to model, rig and animate. Lots of successful cute characters are basically this.

Level 2

🐹 Chibi animal

Big head, bean body, stubby arms and legs, maybe ears and a tail. Needs a simple armature. The most flexible option for gags.

Level 3 · later

🧸 Full character

Fingers, clothes, full body mechanics. Needs Rigify and much more animation skill. Leave it for month 6+.

Originality ruleDon't make "a platypus with an axolotl friend". That's Pio's identity, and it's a company's trademarked brand. Don't use Disney, Sanrio or anime characters in content you want to monetise either. Take the techniques and make a character that's yours. It's also your business asset: you can only sell merch, stickers or licences of a character you own.

8 · Model the character in Blender

We'll build a Level 1–2 character from simple shapes. This method is called primitive + subdivision modelling.

8.1 Set up reference images

  1. New file: File → New → General. Select the default cube and press X → Delete.
  2. Press Numpad 1 (front view). Then Shift+A → Image → Reference → choose your front sketch.
  3. Press Numpad 3 (side view) and add your side sketch the same way.
  4. In the Object properties of each image, lower the Opacity and set the depth to Back, so the image sits behind your model.
  5. Scale the images so the character is about 1–2 metres tall in Blender. Real-ish scale makes lights and physics behave.

8.2 The body

  1. Shift+A → Mesh → UV Sphere. In the small "Adjust Last Operation" panel (bottom left), set Segments 16, Rings 8. Fewer polygons are easier to shape.
  2. Go to Properties → Modifiers (wrench) → Add Modifier → Generate → Subdivision Surface. Set Levels Viewport 2, Render 2. The sphere becomes perfectly smooth.
  3. Right-click the object → Shade Smooth (or Shade Auto Smooth).
  4. Tab into Edit Mode. Press O to turn on Proportional Editing. Moving one vertex now gently pulls its neighbours along (scroll the mouse wheel while moving to change the radius).
  5. Shape the body against your sketches: select the top vertex and pull it up for a taller head, squash the bottom flat so it sits on the ground, widen the cheeks. Check front Numpad 1, side Numpad 3 and top Numpad 7 often.
  6. Symmetry: in Edit Mode, turn on the X mirror (the butterfly icon, top right of the viewport). Edits on one side then copy to the other.
  7. When you're done, Tab back to Object Mode and press Ctrl+A → All Transforms. (It "applies" the scale. Forgetting this causes weird bugs later.)

8.3 Eyes (where the cuteness comes from)

  1. Add a new UV Sphere, scale it small, and flatten it slightly on the Y axis (S Y 0.4) so it sits flush on the face.
  2. Place it below the middle of the face and fairly wide apart.
  3. Add a Mirror modifier (Generate → Mirror) with the body as the Mirror Object. This gives you the second eye automatically.
  4. Highlights: add 1 small sphere (and optionally a tiny second one) in white, on the upper side of each eye. Parent them to the eye: select the highlight, then Shift-click the eye, press Ctrl+P → Object.
  5. Try 3 eye sizes. Bigger is usually cuter, until it gets creepy. Trust the thumbnail test.

8.4 Cheeks, mouth, limbs, accessories

  • Blush: a flattened pink sphere or disc on each cheek (Mirror modifier again). Later you can make it semi-transparent in the material.
  • Mouth, 3 options:
    1. Easiest: a thin curve (Shift+A → Curve → Bezier) with some Bevel Depth under Object Data → Geometry. Bend it into a smile.
    2. Cartoon: draw the mouth with a Grease Pencil object in front of the face. It's 2D, easy to animate, and very charming.
    3. Sculpted: cut the mouth into the mesh and animate it with shape keys (§10). Most work, most expressive.
  • Stubby limbs: a small Capsule (Blender has a capsule mesh; if not, use a UV sphere stretched on Z) for each arm or leg. Mirror across X. Tuck them partly into the body so the joints are hidden.
  • Ears / tail / leaf hat: start from spheres or cones and shape them with proportional editing. Keep them rounded.
  • Name every object in the Outliner (double-click): body, eye.L, arm.L… Future you will thank you.
Low-poly alternative (Vossadova style)Skip the Subdivision modifier, keep the polygons chunky, and use Shade Flat. It's faceted, playful, fast to model and fast to render.
Want to sculpt?Sculpt Mode (the Sculpting tab) is like digital clay and great for soft organic shapes. For your first character, primitives are quicker and easier to rig. Try sculpting in month 3+.

9 · Materials & the cute look

9.1 A soft "clay / vinyl toy" skin (Principled BSDF)

  1. Select the body → Properties → Material (red sphere icon) → New. You get a Principled BSDF material.
  2. Base Color: your main pastel colour.
  3. Roughness: 0.5–0.7 for a soft matte look. (Low roughness looks shiny and plastic.)
  4. Subsurface → Weight: a small value (e.g. 0.05–0.2) lets light glow slightly through the skin, like gummy candy or a baby's ears. Too much looks waxy.
  5. Eyes: a very dark (not pure black) colour with low roughness (0.1–0.2), so they catch a shiny glint.
  6. Blush: pink with some Alpha (under the material's settings, make sure transparency blending is allowed in EEVEE), or use a soft Gradient Texture.
  7. Switch the viewport to Material Preview (Z → Material Preview) to see the colours.

9.2 Optional styles

🎨 Toon / cel-shaded look (flat colour bands like a cartoon)

In the Shading workspace, use the EEVEE render engine and build: Diffuse BSDF → Shader to RGB → Color Ramp (set to Constant, 2–3 colour stops) → Material Output. Adjust the Color Ramp stops to choose where the shadow starts. Search "Blender toon shader EEVEE" for video walk-throughs.

✏️ Outlines

Easy way (inverted hull): duplicate the body, add a Solidify modifier with a small Thickness and Flip normals on, give it a dark material with Backface Culling enabled. Alternative: a Line Art Grease Pencil modifier (Shift+A → Grease Pencil → Scene Line Art).

🧶 Felt / fuzzy look

Add a slight Sheen in the Principled BSDF plus a subtle Noise Texture into the Normal input (through a Bump node). It reads as fabric without rendering real hair.

9.3 Colour tips

  • Background: a slightly contrasting pastel (pink character → mint or sky background).
  • Keep the view transform on AgX (Blender's default, under Render → Color Management). It gives gentle colours and avoids blown-out highlights.
  • Stick to the same palette in every Reel. Viewers learn to recognise your videos by colour alone.

10 · Rigging (making your character movable)

A rig is the set of controls you animate with: an invisible skeleton, plus sliders for facial expressions. Pick the simplest level that works for your character:

10.1 Level A: no skeleton at all (blob characters)

  1. Move the origin to the bottom: in Edit Mode, select the lowest vertex → Shift+S → Cursor to Selected. Back in Object Mode: Object → Set Origin → Origin to 3D Cursor. Now scaling squashes the character onto the floor instead of into the air.
  2. Parent the eyes, cheeks and mouth to the body (select them all, then the body last → Ctrl+P → Object). Now they move with it.
  3. Squash & stretch: just animate the body's Scale (e.g. Z 0.8, X/Y 1.12 for a squash). Keep the volume roughly constant: when it gets shorter, it gets wider.
  4. Bend and wobble: add a Simple Deform modifier (Bend or Taper) or a Lattice (Shift+A → Lattice, then add a Lattice modifier to the body). Animate the lattice points for jelly wobbles.

10.2 Level B: a simple armature (chibi animals)

  1. Object Mode → Shift+A → Armature. It adds one bone. Tab into Edit Mode on the armature.
  2. Put the first bone in the body/hips (name it root), then select its tip and E to extrude a spine, then a head bone.
  3. Extrude arms and legs from the right places (one side only), name them arm.L, leg.L… then Armature → Symmetrize to create the .R side.
  4. In Object Mode, select the mesh(es), then the armature last → Ctrl+P → With Automatic Weights.
  5. Test it: select the armature → Pose Mode (Ctrl+Tab) → rotate bones with R. If a part moves wrongly, fix it in Weight Paint mode (red = fully follows the bone, blue = ignores it).
  6. Separate rigid parts (eyes, hat) can simply be parented to a specific bone: select the object, then the armature, go into Pose Mode, select the bone → Ctrl+P → Bone.
  7. Look-at eyes: add an Empty in front of the face, then give each eye a Damped Track (or Track To) constraint targeting it. Move the empty and the eyes follow. Very useful for "looks at camera" moments.

10.3 Level C: Rigify (full characters, later)

Blender ships with Rigify (enable it in Preferences → Add-ons / Extensions if it isn't on). Add a meta-rig, fit it to your character, and click Generate Rig to get pro-level controls. Great, but it's overkill for blobs and chibis at the start.

10.4 Facial expressions with shape keys (all levels)

  1. Select the body mesh → Object Data (green triangle) → Shape Keys → +. The first key is Basis (the neutral face). Click + again to add Key 1 and rename it, e.g. blink.
  2. With blink selected, Tab into Edit Mode and model the eyes closed (e.g. flatten the eye spheres, or move eyelid geometry). Tab out.
  3. Now its Value slider (0 → 1) morphs between neutral and blink. Hover over the slider and press I to keyframe it.
  4. Make this starter set: blink, smile, mouth_O (surprise), sad_brows, squint_happy (^_^ eyes), cheeks_puff.
  5. Separate eye objects? Give each eye its own shape keys (e.g. scale the eye flat for a blink). Or simply animate the eye's Scale Z, which works surprisingly well.
2D faces are a legit shortcutMany cute channels use drawn (Grease Pencil) or swappable texture faces: ^_^, >_<, T_T. They're very expressive, anime-like, and quick to animate. You can mix them: 3D body, 2D face.

Save the rigged character as CHAR_<name>_v01.blend. In every new Reel, bring it in with File → Link (or Append) plus Library Override, or just start each Reel from your studio template file (§17).

11 · Animation: making it alive

A keyframe is a saved pose at a specific frame. Blender fills in the frames between keyframes automatically (this is called interpolation). Animation means choosing which poses and when. That's it. The craft is in the timing.

11.1 See the difference yourself

❌ Linear: robotic
🙂 Eased: natural
🥰 Eased + squash & stretch: cute

11.2 The 8 principles that matter most for cute

These come from the classic "12 principles of animation" by Disney animators Ollie Johnston and Frank Thomas.

PrincipleMeaningCute example
Squash & stretchSoft things deform when they move or hit something, keeping the same volumeSquash before a hop, stretch in the air, squash on landing
AnticipationA small opposite move before the main actionCrouch down before jumping; lean back before a sneeze
TimingNumber of frames = weight and emotionFast = light and excited; slow = sleepy and sad
Ease in / ease outThings speed up and slow down graduallyBlender does this by default (Bézier interpolation)
Overshoot & settleGo a little past the final pose, then come backA head turn goes 10° too far, then wobbles back
Follow-through / overlapLoose parts (ears, tail, hat) lag behind and keep moving after the body stopsEars flop 2–4 frames after the head
Secondary actionA small extra action that supports the main oneA blink or a tail wag during a pause
Appeal & exaggerationPush poses further than realityA "shocked" pose where the whole body stretches up

11.3 Setting up for animation

  1. Output Properties (printer icon): Frame Rate 30 fps. That's the platforms' recommended rate. Stick with it for every Reel.
  2. Set the frame range: Start 1, End = seconds × 30 (e.g. 8 s → End 240).
  3. Switch to the Animation workspace tab. It has the Dope Sheet (keyframes as diamonds) and a camera view.
  4. Film yourself on your phone acting the move out (hop, flinch, dance). Real reference makes animation much better, even for a blob.

11.4 Your first character animation: the "jelly hop" (30 fps, Level A rig)

The origin is at the character's feet (see §10.1). Select the body. At each frame, set the values in the N-panel, hover over them and press I to keyframe:

FramePoseLocation ZScale X / YScale Z

(Location Z is in Blender units. Scale it to your character's size: the jump should be about 1× the character's height.)

  • Add a blink on the shape key during the hold: frame 36 value 0 → 38 value 1 → 39 value 1 → 42 value 0.
  • Ears/limbs: copy the body's timing but shift it 2–3 frames later in the Dope Sheet (that's follow-through).
  • Contact shadow: have a small dark disc (or just good lighting) under the character. It shrinks when the character is in the air.

11.5 The pro workflow: block → spline → polish

  1. Blocking: key only the main poses (key poses). In the Dope Sheet select all keys → T → Constant, so poses "pop" from one to the next. Judge the poses and timing only. Is the story clear?
  2. Splining: select all → T → Bézier. Blender now makes smooth in-betweens. It will feel floaty at first. That's normal.
  3. Polish in the Graph Editor (change any panel's editor type to Graph Editor). Each curve shows one value over time. Steep = fast, flat = slow/still. Drag the handles to make contacts snappy (sharp corners at landings) and hang-time soft (rounded tops).
  4. Seamless loops: make the last key identical to the first (copy it: Ctrl+C / Ctrl+V), and/or in the Graph Editor use Channel → Extrapolation Mode → Make Cyclic (Shift+E). A loop that's hard to spot gets more replays.
  5. Hold poses: let important expressions stay on screen for at least 15 frames (½ s) so viewers can read them. Add a tiny movement (breathing, a 1% scale pulse) so the hold doesn't look frozen.

11.7 Animating to audio (trending sounds, lip-sync, beats)

  1. Cut your audio clip first (Audacity) to the exact length, and export it as WAV.
  2. In Blender, open a Video Sequencer editor and Add → Sound. Place it at frame 1. Turn on Display Waveform in the strip's settings to see the peaks.
  3. In the Timeline's Playback menu, set Sync → Sync to Audio (older versions call it "AV-sync" or "Frame Dropping"), and enable Scrubbing so you hear sound while dragging the time.
  4. Play it and tap M on every beat or syllable to drop a marker. Name markers (F2) like "BOOM" or "huh".
  5. Put the key poses on the markers. Accent hits look best when the pose lands 1–2 frames before the sound. The brain perceives this as in sync.
  6. Lip-sync basics: you don't need every sound. Use only 3 mouth shapes: closed (M, B, P), open (A), round (O/U). Open the mouth on loud syllables, close it on pauses. Cute characters read better with less mouth movement.
  7. The audio in Blender is only for timing. You'll do the final mix in Resolve (§15).

12 · Camera, lighting & vertical framing

12.1 Vertical camera

  1. Output Properties → Resolution X 1080, Y 1920 (9:16), 100%.
  2. Select the camera → Camera properties → Focal Length 50–85 mm. Longer lenses flatter cute faces. Use a wide lens (20–24 mm) close to the face for comedy "fish-eye" shots.
  3. Look through the camera (Numpad 0). Frame the shot the way you like in the normal view, then Ctrl+Alt+Numpad 0 to snap the camera there.
  4. Camera properties → Viewport Display → Composition Guides → turn on Thirds and Center to help with framing.
  5. Put the camera at the character's eye level or slightly below. Looking down on a character makes it look small and vulnerable (cute). Looking up makes it look heroic.

12.2 Safe zones: where the app's buttons will cover your video

top: username / tabs
buttons
caption, audio, profile
Keep the face, gag & text in here

Approximate. Each app's overlays differ a bit. Roughly: keep key content out of the top ~12%, the bottom ~20–25%, and the right-hand ~14% of the frame. Preview on your phone before posting.

12.3 Soft "product-shot" lighting (3 lights)

  1. Delete the default light. Add → Light → Area lights (they give soft, flattering shadows).
  2. Key light: front-left, a bit above, big (Size 2–3 m), warm white. This is the main light.
  3. Fill light: front-right, about ⅓ the power of the key, slightly cool. It softens the shadows.
  4. Rim light: behind the character, pointing at its back and edges. It creates a bright outline that separates the character from the background. This is the secret to a "premium" look.
  5. World: World properties → set Color to a soft pastel and strength ~0.5–1, for gentle ambient light.
  6. Ground: a plane with a gently curved back wall (a "cyclorama": a plane with one edge extruded up and bevelled) in your background colour. There's no horizon line, so it looks like a toy studio.

12.4 Depth of field (the blurry background)

Camera properties → Depth of Field ON, Focus Object = the character's head, F-Stop 2–4. A blurry background makes things look tiny and toy-like (the "miniature" effect). Very cute.

LookGreat for stylised / toon / clayBest for realistic light, glass, soft shadows

13.1 EEVEE settings for a Reel

  1. Render Properties → Render Engine → EEVEE. Render samples around 32–64. Enable Motion Blur (it makes fast motion smoother).
  2. Try raising shadow and ray-tracing quality only if something looks wrong. Defaults are usually fine for stylised scenes.
  3. Output Properties → Output: set a folder like //render/ (the // means "next to the .blend file").
  4. File Format: PNG (an image sequence, one image per frame). If Blender crashes at frame 180, you keep frames 1–179 and can restart from 180. Rendering straight to MP4 would lose everything.
  5. Press F12 to test-render one frame. Check it looks right. Then Render → Render Animation (Ctrl+F12).
  6. Rough time: 240 frames × 3–10 s per frame in EEVEE ≈ 12–40 minutes on a mid-range PC. Render while you eat or sleep.
Faster previewsFor quick timing checks, use View → Viewport Render Animation. It records exactly what the viewport shows, in seconds. Perfect for checking the animation before the real render.

14 · Sound: the other 50% of cute

Play a cute animation muted and it drops from 10/10 to 6/10. Pio-style channels lean on trending audio and small, perfectly timed sound effects. Sound is not an afterthought.

14.1 The four sound layers

LayerExamplesTips
Sound effects (SFX)pop, boing, squeak, pitter-patter, whoosh, sparkle "ting", bubble, munch, thudPut one on almost every action. Softer and higher-pitched = cuter.
Character voiceGibberish "mip!", "hehe", gasps, sighs, sneezesYour own voice, pitched up (see below). No real words means it works in every language, which suits a global audience.
Music bedLight ukulele, glockenspiel, lo-fiKeep it quiet under the SFX
Trending audioA popular meme sound or song clipAdd it inside the Instagram app from its music library, not baked into your file (see licensing below)

14.2 Build a starter SFX library (30 sounds)

  1. Pixabay Sound Effects (pixabay.com/sound-effects): free under Pixabay's content licence. Search "cartoon boing", "pop", "squeak", "cute". Read its licence page once.
  2. Freesound.org: a huge library, but each sound has its own licence. CC0 = use freely. CC-BY = you must credit the author (put credits in the caption or description). Avoid CC-BY-NC (non-commercial) because you're planning to earn.
  3. YouTube Audio Library (in YouTube Studio → Audio Library): free music and SFX for YouTube videos.
  4. Meta Sound Collection: royalty-free tracks and SFX for Facebook and Instagram use.
  5. Record your own (foley): it's free, 100% yours, and unique. Pop your lips (cute "pop"), flick a filled water bottle, crinkle a chip packet, tap fingernails on a table (tiny footsteps), bubble wrap, a rubber band "boing". Record on your phone under a blanket to kill echo.
  6. Save everything into folders: SFX/pops, SFX/boings, SFX/steps, SFX/voice… plus a LICENCES.txt noting where each file came from.

14.3 Make your character's voice (Audacity)

  1. Record yourself saying gibberish lines, gasps and giggles (File → New, then the red Record button). Do 5 takes of each.
  2. Select the good take → Effect → Noise Removal and Repair → Noise Reduction. First select a silent bit and click "Get Noise Profile", then select everything and apply.
  3. Effect → Pitch and Tempo → Change Pitch: raise it by +4 to +8 semitones. More gets chipmunk-y. Find what fits your character and always use the same value, so the voice is recognisable.
  4. Optionally speed it up slightly (Change Tempo +5–10%).
  5. File → Export Audio → WAV.

14.4 Music licensing: the rules that protect your account

Important
  • Instagram: commercially released songs from the in-app music library are licensed for personal and creator use inside Instagram. Business accounts get a limited library, and branded or paid-partnership posts need properly cleared music. So choose a Creator account (see §18).
  • Don't bake copyrighted songs into your exported video and upload it to YouTube. Content ID will match it, and the ad money can go to the rights holder or the video can be blocked. For YouTube, use your own sounds, the YouTube Audio Library, or Shorts' own in-app audio picker.
  • There's no "10-second rule". Short clips of copyrighted music are still copyrighted.
  • Best long-term play: original sounds. If your character's "mip!" becomes a sound other people use, the sound's page on Instagram links back to you. That's free promotion.

15 · Editing the Reel / Short (DaVinci Resolve, free)

15.1 One-time project setup

  1. Open Resolve → Project Manager → New Project ("Channel_Reels").
  2. Gear icon (bottom right) → Master Settings: Timeline resolution Custom 1080 × 1920 ("for vertical"), Timeline frame rate 30, Playback 30. Save. (Tip: once it's set, you can save these as the default project settings.)
  3. Preferences → User → Editing: set the standard still duration if you use pictures (optional).

15.2 Each Reel

  1. Media page → drag in your render folder. Resolve normally detects the PNG sequence as one clip. (If you see hundreds of separate images instead, open the Media Pool's ⋯ options menu and switch off "Show Individual Frames".)
  2. Edit page → drag the clip onto the timeline. Drag your SFX and voice WAVs onto audio tracks A1, A2, A3 (e.g. A1 voice, A2 SFX, A3 music).
  3. Line up every SFX with its action frame by frame. Use ← / → to step one frame at a time. Nudge SFX 1–2 frames early if a hit feels late.
  4. Text: Effects → Titles → Text+. Use a short hook ("when the snack is gone 😭") inside the safe zone, in a rounded bold font, white with a dark outline. Keep it on screen long enough to read (about 1 s per 3–4 words).
  5. Captions (if there's voice or words): the Subtitle track, or add them in-app when posting.
  6. Fairlight page (the audio page): watch the Loudness meter. A common short-form target is about −14 LUFS integrated, with peaks no higher than about −1 dBTP. Rough mix: voice loudest, SFX a little under, music well under (around −18 to −24 dB relative).
  7. Loop check: set in/out points at the start and end and play on loop (Ctrl+/). Does it flow smoothly back to the start?
  8. Deliver page → Custom export: MP4, H.264, 1080×1920, 30 fps, quality/bitrate around 10–16 Mb/s, audio AAC, 320 kb/s. Add to Render Queue → Render All.
  9. Copy it to your phone (Google Drive / USB) and watch it on the phone before posting. That's how your audience sees it.
Platform specs at a glance (Sept 2026)9:16, 1080×1920, H.264 + AAC, 30 fps. Instagram accepts much longer uploads, but Reels over 3 minutes aren't recommended to non-followers, and your micro-animations will be 5–20 s anyway. YouTube Shorts can be up to 3 minutes.

16.2 Eight content pillars (rotate them)

🔥 1. Trend recreation

Monday mornings, exams, waiting for food delivery, low phone battery, "5 more minutes". These are the most sendable type.

👆 3. Interactive

"Pause to pick a snack", "tap to wake him up", "play along to the beat". These drive comments and replays.

🍜 4. Food & ASMR

Tiny character + giant food, with crunchy and squishy sounds. Simple, satisfying, universally understood.

🎉 5. Seasonal & festival

Diwali lamps, Holi colours, monsoon puddles, Christmas, birthdays. Plan them 2 weeks early.

👯 6. Duo gags

🔁 7. Perfect loops

Idle bounces, sleeping breaths, a dance loop. Cheap to make and great for replays.

🛠️ 8. Behind the scenes

Viewport → final, "how I made this in Blender", the raddoob-style camera cheat, the Kensyouen_Y-style "character notices the animator". These also attract potential clients.

20 · Using AI without getting punished

AI is useful as an assistant. It's risky as the author.

✅ Good uses

  • Brainstorming gag ideas and hook text
  • Explaining Blender errors ("why is my render black?")
  • Writing small Blender Python scripts (e.g. "randomise the blink timing")
  • Planning, captions, translations
  • DaVinci Resolve 21.1 can connect to AI assistants to organise media and batch render

⚠️ Risky uses

  • Fully AI-generated video presented as your animation
  • Mass-producing near-identical Reels from one template
  • AI voices cloned from real people
Platform rulesYouTube renamed its "repetitious content" rule to "inauthentic content" in July 2025. In July 2026 it clarified three categories that can't be monetised: generic, repetitive or template-based content; off-putting or distressing content; and AI personas discussing sensitive topics such as health and finance. Instagram also pushes originality. Your defence is real, human-made, varied animation. Reusing your rig is fine. Posting the same gag 30 times with a different colour is not. Label realistic AI content where the platforms ask you to.

21 · Copyright, kids & tax basics

  • Your character is your IP. Keep your sketches and dated .blend files as a record that you created it. Consider a trademark on the name later if it grows (ask a professional).
  • Memes and trends: recreating a meme in your own animation is common, but it's not automatically legal, especially for sponsored posts. Don't use copyrighted characters, logos or real people's likenesses in paid work.
  • Sound licences: keep LICENCES.txt, credit CC-BY sounds, and avoid NC ("non-commercial") licences (§14).
  • "Made for kids" (YouTube): cute animation can attract children. YouTube requires every creator to say whether a video is made for kids. If it's aimed at children (toddler-style visuals, nursery themes), you must mark it, and that turns off comments, personalised ads and some features. If you target teens and adults (relatable humour, office and exam jokes), say so and keep themes teen/adult. When unsure, read YouTube's "Determining if your content is made for kids" help page.
  • Tax (India): platform and client income is taxable income. YouTube pays through AdSense from abroad. Submit your tax info in AdSense so US withholding on US-viewer earnings is handled correctly. GST registration, export-of-services rules and ITR filing depend on your income and situation, so talk to a CA once money starts coming in. This guide is not tax advice.

22 · Troubleshooting: the beginner problems everyone hits

ProblemLikely cause → fix
Render is completely blackNo light, or the camera faces the wrong way → add lights, press Numpad 0 and check the view. Also check the Outliner camera/render icons aren't turned off.
Squash happens "in the air" or the character sinks into the floorThe origin isn't at the feet → §10.1 step 1
Modifiers or rig behave strangely, bevels are unevenScale not applied → Ctrl+A → All Transforms (before rigging)
Dark patches / weird shading on the modelFlipped normals → Edit Mode, select all, Shift+N (Recalculate Outside)
Parts of the mesh stretch wrongly when a bone movesBad automatic weights → fix in Weight Paint mode, or separate rigid parts and parent them to bones
Animation feels floaty / like it's underwaterToo many evenly spaced keys and soft curves everywhere → sharper contacts in the Graph Editor, fewer in-betweens, add holds
Animation feels stiffNo overlap → offset ears, tail and hat 2–4 frames. Add a blink and breathing.
Audio drifts out of sync in BlenderPlayback sync mode → set it to Sync to Audio (§11.7). Final sync is checked in Resolve.
Rendering takes foreverUsing Cycles → switch to EEVEE, lower the samples, disable things you can't see, render at 100% 1080×1920 (not 4K)
Exported MP4 has no soundAudio export disabled in Deliver → tick "Export Audio" (AAC)
Instagram makes the video blurryUpload over Wi-Fi. Check in the app settings that high-quality upload is enabled. Export at exactly 1080×1920, 30 fps.
Song is "unavailable" on InstagramBusiness account or region → switch to a Creator account, or use original audio / the Sound Collection
Blender crashed and you lost workFile → Recover → Auto Save / Last Session. Turn on auto-save (§6.2).

24 · Glossary

TermPlain meaning
Mesh / vertex / edge / faceA 3D shape made of points (vertices), lines between them (edges) and surfaces (faces)
ModifierA non-destructive effect on an object (smoothing, mirroring, bending)
Subdivision SurfaceA modifier that makes a blocky mesh smooth and round
OriginThe orange dot: the point an object moves, rotates and scales around
Material / shaderHow a surface looks: colour, shininess, softness
Rig / armature / boneThe invisible skeleton and controls used to pose a character
Weight paintingPainting how strongly each bone moves each part of the mesh
Shape keyA saved alternative shape of a mesh (like a smile) that you can blend in with a slider
KeyframeA saved value at a specific frame. Blender fills in the frames between keys.
InterpolationHow Blender fills between keys: Constant (jumps), Linear, Bézier (eased)
Dope Sheet / Graph EditorTimeline views of keyframes: as diamonds (timing) or as curves (speed)
Blocking / splining / polishStages of animation: key poses → smooth motion → fine detail
EEVEE / CyclesBlender's fast real-time renderer / its slower realistic renderer
Image sequenceA render saved as one image per frame (crash-safe), combined into video later
Grease PencilBlender's tool for 2D drawing and animation inside 3D scenes
SFX / foleySound effects / sound effects you record yourself with everyday objects
